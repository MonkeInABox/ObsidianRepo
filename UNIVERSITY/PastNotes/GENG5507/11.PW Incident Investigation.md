# Failure
- Something going wrong, not working as planned
- Types:
	- Human (errors)
	- Organisational
	- Technical (faults)
- **Accident**: Event where a failure or combination of several failures eventually leads to at least some undesired negative consequences
- **Near Miss**: Event where an accident could have occurred, but all undesired negative consequences have been avoided
- **Incident**: Term covers both accidents and near misses, frequently used for severity levels in between accident and near miss
## Causal Factors and Root Causes
- **Causal Factors**: Those contributing factors that if eliminated would have either prevented the incident or reduced its severity. 
	- Also referred to as immediate or direct causes, not to be confused with root causes
	- Incidents are usually the result of multiple
	- A causal factor may have several root causes
- **Root Causes**: Deeper underlying causes, explaining why the immediate causal factors could happen. 
	- Contribute to causal factors directly or via intermediate causes
	- Repeatedly asking what led to a higher level cause
	- Investigation stopping rule: remain within organisations control
## Active vs. Latent Failures
- **Active Failures/Errors**: Occur at sharp end of operations with immediate effects
	- Similarities with definition of causal factors
	- Mainly technical faults and human error
- **Latent Failures**: Their effects lie dormant for a long time and only become evident when combined with other factors
	- Mainly human factors issues and organisational failures

# Incident Causation
- Investigations are usually guided by a theory or model of how incidents can occur
- The model determines: 
	- Which factors the investigators look for
	- Where and when the investigation starts and stops
	- The quality of recommendations for future prevention of/protection against recurrence of the incident
## History 
- Focus of incident causation models has changed over time
	- From technical faults/failures, to unsafe acts and human error, to human factors influencing human error, to underlying (latent) organisational factors including culture
- Change in model complexity over time
	- Single root cause models
	- Extended root cause models
	- Multi-cause joint effects models
	- Extended multi-cause joint effects models
	- Models allowing a nonlinear combination of factors and interactions

## Single vs. Extended Root Cause Model
- Single: 
	- Root cause -> Accident 
	- Tends to halt investigation at symptom level, often favours human error, assigns blame
- Extended:
	- Root Cause -> Cause -> Cause -> Accident
	- Acknowledges need to dig for deeper underlying cause, ignores possibility of multiple concurrent contributing factors

## Complex non-linear models
- Often modern organisations are complex, dynamic socio-technical systems with tightly coupled non-linear processes
- Incidents in such systems can not accurately be described with linear accident causation models
- Two recent attempts to overcome this issue:
	- Systems Theoretic Accident Model and Processes (STAMP)
		- Systems are interrelated components kept in dynamic equilibrium by feedback loops of information and control
		- Safety management systems are required to continuously control tasks and impose constraints to ensure system safety
		- Accident investigations focuses on why the controls that were in place failed to detect or prevent changes that ultimately lead to an accident
	- Functional Resonance Analysis Method (FRAM)
		- Purpose is to:
			- Identify how the system should have functioned for everything to succeed (everyday performance)
			- Understand the variability of functions which alone or in combination prevented that from happening
# Root Cause Analysis (RCA)
- A process designed for use in investigating and categorising the root causes of events with safety, health, environmental, quality, reliability and production impacts
- RCA is a tool for continuous improvement
- Supported by categorisation systems (taxonomies) of various types of root causes
- Trending over time for frequently recurring (combinations) root causes
- When performed after an incident has occurred, RCA is a reactive method
- When used to audit systems and processes, RCA becomes a proactive method, enabling the user to forecast the possibility of an incident/undesired event before it occurs
## Steps Involved in Incident Investigation
1. Immediate incident scene management
	- Take any necessary action to prevent further escalation
	- Secure the scene
	- Start collecting information as soon as possible
	- Deal with legal requirements
2. Set up investigation team
	- Involve various expertise
	- When contractors involved them in team
3. Evidence Collection
	- Photos, videos, sketches, measurements, samples
	- Physical environment at the time of the incident
	- Interviews with witnesses and others who may have insights
	- Analysis/testing of any failed equipment, parts and materials
	- Applicable rules/standards/procedures
		- SWPs
		- OEM manuals
		- Records
4. Analyse Data
	- Establish event time sequence
	- May identify need for further information collection
	- Use suitable causation model
	- Identify root causes
5. Develop recommendations
6. Record, report and present investigation findings and recommendations
7. Implement actions
8. Review actions
9. Capture and disseminate broader lessons learnt

## Fault Tree Analysis
- Logic pathways to connect underlying causes to higher level causes
- Causes connecting through an `AND` gate are collectively necessary and sufficient to produce the higher level cause
- Cause connecting through an `OR` gate to a higher level event are individually sufficient
![[Screenshot_20260603_154320.png|559]]![[Screenshot_20260603_154331.png]]
- Time and resource consuming
- Does not allow for interactions between different branches of the tree
- Only includes faults (and some versions also errors), not context
- Difficult to include information regarding timing and duration
- Quality depends on investigators knowledge
## Causal Tree Analysis
- Derived from and very similar to FTA
- Allows inclusion of situational factors that contributed but are not necessarily faults or failures
- Mainly used retrospective, no probability calculations needed
- Most versions only used `AND` gates![[Screenshot_20260603_154719.png]]
- It can incorporate non-failing context
# Sequentially Timed Event Plotting (STEP)
- A STEP worksheet structures the analysis:
	- Actors involved in the incident are listed down the vertical axis
	- A timeline is established on the horizontal axis
- Identify the main events/actions that contributed to the accident to construct event building blocks which may contain the following information
	1. Time at which the event and action started
	2. Duration of the event and action
	3. Agent which caused the event or action
	4. Description of the event or action
	5. Name of the source which offered this information
![[Screenshot_20260603_155101.png|697]]
- Difficult to represent human factors issues and organisational factors that influenced events
- Time and resource consuming (when used for complex accidents)
## TapRooT
- Incorporates human factors
- Includes:
	- Snapchart: a visual technique for collecting and organising information about events, sequence and details/conditions surrounding events, to understand what happened, like an event time sequence with additional information for some events
	- Root cause tree: a systematic, repeatable way to find the root causes of human performance and equipment problems (aka causal factors) identified in the snapchart 
	- Corrective action helper: helps investigators develop effective corrective actions
- Defines a root cause as 'the absence of a best practice or the failure to apply knowledge that would have prevented the problem'
- Defines root cause analysis as 'the search for the best practices and/or the missing knowledge that will keep a problem from recurring'
- With this positive spin on the purpose of doing a root cause analysis, along with its strong focus on human factors underlying errors, it helps to steer away from assigning blame
![[Screenshot_20260603_160102.png]]
- Investigators will not find factors that are not included in the root cause tree (although developers insist it is more than a mere checklist, with questions guiding users to areas of the tree)
- Up to user to decide which events and/or surrounding conditions are causal factors that need further investigation - so quality still depends on investigator

## Determining Causal Factors and Root Causes
- Identify those events/conditions that form the direct causes of the incident - these are the causal factors
- For each of those causal factors, use the root cause categories checklist to dig for deeper underlying causes, to identify applicable root causes
- Use checklist to identify root causes for any other significant issues identified in the chart
- Checklist can also be used to check if any applicable problems have been overlooked in the chart
- Note that the checklist is not exhaustive, one should not restrict oneself to only looking for those causes included in the checklist
