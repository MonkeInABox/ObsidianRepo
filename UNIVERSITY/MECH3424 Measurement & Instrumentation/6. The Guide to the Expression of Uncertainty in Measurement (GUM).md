# GUM Methods
- Provide a repeatable approach to defining all possible sources of error which lead to uncertainty in th experiment or system
- IGNORE what you think is the dominate source of error, define and then quantify all uncerainties
- Once all have been enalysed, the dominant can be identified and corrective actions can be taken to reduce the uncertainty $U$ in $\bar{y} \pm U$
- We want to find the total uncertainty $u_c$ for the output property $\bar{y}$, requiring:
	- A list of all errors, each source of error indexed by $i$
	- A list of all $u_i$ the standard uncertainty of the source of errors
	- A sensitivity $c_i$ equivalent to the slope of the data/graph or derivative $c_{i} = \frac{\delta y}{\delta u_{i}}$
	- An effective degree of freedom $v_{eff}$ for the final parameter $u_c$ (think of $v_{eff}=n_{eff}-1$ where $n_{eff}$ is equivalent to the estimated number of points that would need to be measured in the final parameter $y_i$ to get the same result)
	- A factor $k$ that tells us how to scale the total uncertainty $u_c$ to be 95% confident in your final uncertainty
$$\bar{y} \pm ku_{c}=\bar{y}\pm u$$
# Measurement Errors and Uncertainties
- Measurement is the process of assigning a value to a physical variable based on sampling data from the population of a variable
- Errors are a property of the measurement. They are an effect that causes a mesaured value to differ from the true value
- Uncertainty is a property of the result. It is assigned a numerical value that quantifies the probable range of errors
- Best estimate of $x$ is the sample mean $\bar{x}$ and the uncertainty in the value $$x'=\bar{x}\pm u_{x}$$
- Systematic errors shift the sample mean away from the true mean by a fixed amount. 
- With many measurements, random errors result in a distribution of values about the sample mean
# Types of Distributions
- No longer refer to systematic errors
- Each input $x_i$ and its associated standard uncertainties $u(x_i)$ are obtained from a distribution of possible values of the input quality $X_i$ 
- **Type A**
	- Described as a frequency (histogram) based distribution
	- Obtained from a series of $k$ observations $X_{i,k}$ (the kth observation of $X_{i}$) or $X_{i}$ (the input quantity)
	- Evaluated by a statistical analysis (due to a random variable)

- **Type B**
	- Described as a priori distribution (information available from sources other than a statistical investigation, in particular, expert judgement, past experience, or prior belief)
	- Insideous uncertainties, there is nothing in a measurement to suggest their prescence. Where possible B uncertainties should be eliminated or reduced. Where this cannot be done, they should be quanitified and treated in the same manner as A uncertainties

## Input Quantities
- GUM denotes $X_i$ is the input (mass, velocity, pressure) and the output is $Y$ (density, position, acceleration)
## Trueness and Precision 
- If an experiment has low type B uncertainty, it is said to be true
- If an experiment has low type A uncertainty, it is said to be precise
- Low accuracy from a high precision instrument are usually caused by an offset $\Delta$ in the instrument and can be corrected with calibration (minimising B uncertainty)
# Means and Standard Deviations
- The best estimate for the expected value of $\mu_v$ of quantity $V$ that varies randomly (it is a random variable) is the average 
- If $n$ independent observations $V_k$ have been obtained under the same conditions then this is given as $$\bar{V} = \frac{1}{n}\Sigma^{n}_{k=1}V_{k}=\bar{X_{i}}$$
- Individual values of $V_k$ differ due to random variations. The experimental variance (an estimation of the variance $\sigma^{2}$ of $V$) is: $$s^{2}(V_k)=\frac{1}{n-1}\Sigma^{n}_{j=1}(V_{j}-\bar{V})^{2}$$
- Note the $n-1$ term. Since the same $n$ values of $V_k$ have been used to calculate $\bar{V}$ as: $$n\bar{V} = (V_{1}+V_{2}+...V_{n})$$
- Then by using $\bar{V}$ in the calculation of $s^2$, we no longer have $n$ independent values of $V_k$ and we account for this by using only $n-1$ values
## Standard Deviation of Mean
- $s(V_k)$ is the standard deviation from the raw measurements of $V_k$ 
- Define the standard deviation of the mean (ESDM) as $$ESDM = s(\bar{V})=\sqrt{\frac{s^{2}(V_{k})}{n}}=u_A$$
- It turns out that $s^{2}(\bar{V})$ is the best estimate of the variance of the mean $\sigma^{2}(\bar{V})=\frac{\sigma^{2}}{n}$
- The ESDM is valid for A uncertainties
- The experimental variance of the mean $s^{2}(\bar{V})$ quantifies how well $\bar{V}$ estimates the expectation $\mu$ and may be used as a measure of the uncertainties of $\bar{V}$ so that from n-independent repeat observations
- Variation of means from distinct measurement (with no B uncertainties) 
- The variance of the mean = ESDM$^2$ 
### Difference Between SD and ESDM
- SD quantifies scalar - how much the values vary from one another
- ESDM quantifies how precisely you know the true mean of the population. It takes into account both the value of SD and the sample size
- Both SD and ESDM are in the same units - units of the data
- The ESDM, is always smaller than the SD
- The ESDM gets smaller as your samples get larger
- The SD does not change predictably as you acquire more data, the SD you compute from a sample is the best possible estimate of the SD of the overall population. 
	- As you collect more data, you'll assess the SD of the population wiht more precision, but you can't predict whether of the SD from a larger sample will be bigger or smaller than the SD from a small sample
# Output Quantities
- The aim of the ISO GUM is to “advocate the use of the combined standard uncertainty $u_{c}(y)$ as the parameter for expressing quantitatively the uncertainty of the result of a measurement” and that it is used “by all participants in giving the results of all international comparisons”
- Consider an output (measurand) $Y$ which is not directly measured but depends on $N$ other quantities $X$ given by: $$Y=f(X_{1}, X_{2}, ... X_{n})$$
- E.g. For a temperature dependent resistor, the Power (P) across the resistor is given by: $$Y=f(V,R_{0},\alpha, T)=\frac{V^{2}}{R_{0}(1+\alpha(T-T_{0}))}$$
# Input Quantities
$$Y=f(V,R_{0},\alpha, T)=\frac{V^{2}}{R_{0}(1+\alpha(T-T_{0}))}$$
- Where $X_{1},X_{2}, ...X_{n}=V, R_{0}, \alpha, T$ are the input quantities
- Note, that while these are considered as input quantities, they may also be the outputs (measurands) of other measurements or depend on other quantities
- A full description of the measurand $Y$ may be very difficult to achieve
- In general, input quantities:
	- Whose values and uncertainties can be directly determined in the current measurement (example, $T$ in above)
	- Whose values and uncertainties come from external sources such as calibrations of constants (example, $\alpha$ in above)

## Modeling to Obtain Combined Uncertainty
- When all the uncertainties from A and B have been combined, the result is the Combined standard uncertainty
- Low type B uncertainty it is said to be true (low bias) = $u_B$ 
- Low type A uncertainty it is said to be precise = $u_A$ 
- $u_{x}(y)=\sqrt{u_{A}^{2}+u_{B}^{2}}$ here the accuracy is the combined standard uncertainty

# Determining $u_{c}(y)$ - Uncorrelated Inputs
- Based on the Law of Propagation of Uncertainties (UPL) $$u_{c}^{2}(y) = \Sigma^{N}_{i=1}u_{i}^{2}(y)=\Sigma^{N}_{i=1}(\frac{\delta f}{\delta x_{i}})^{2}u^{2}(x_{i})$$
- All unput quantities must be independent
- To solve for $u_c(y)$ the measurand $Y$ must be able to be modelled as a function $y=f(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{n})$
# Combined Uncertainties - Special Cases
- When the measurand is a linear function of the input quantities such that: $y=c_{1}X_{1}+...+c_{N}X_{N}$ $$u_{c}^{2}(y) = \Sigma^{N}_{i=1}(c_{i}u(x_{i}))^{2}$$
- And where $c=\pm 1$ then: $$u_{x}^{2}(y) = \Sigma ^{N}_{i=1}u(x_{i})^2$$
- When measurand is the product of input quantities raised to an exponent $p_i$ then $y=c(X_{1})^{p1}...(X_{N})^{pN}$
- And the combined variance is $[u_{c}(y)/y]^{2}=\Sigma^{N}_{i=1}[\frac{p_{i}ux_{i}}{x_{i}}]^2$
	- Or the relative combined variance = the relative variance

# Correlation Coefficient
- The (cross) correlation coefficient $$\rho (x_{i},x_{j})=\frac{u(x_{i},x_{j})}{u(x_{i})u(x_{j})}$$ is often easier to understand than the covariance $u(x_{i},x_{j})$
- Range: $-1\leq \rho(x_{i},x_{j})\leq +1$
- If $0$, $x_{i},x_{j}$ are independent
- If 1, $x_{i},x_{j}$ are identical
- If -1, $x_{i},x_{j}$ are identical opposites

# Expanded Uncertainty
- The combined uncertainty $u_{c}(y)$ can universally describe the uncertainty of a measurement. However, in many applications it is required to give a measure of the uncertainty that defines an inerval about the measurement in which a large fraction of the values would lie. This is provided by the expanded uncertainty given by: $$U=ku_{c}(y)$$
- Where $k$ is defined as the coverage factor. The measured result can then be expressed as $Y=t\pm U$
- Do not used confidence intervals, instead used level of confidence
- Confidence intervals have a well defined meaning is statistics but this term is not to be used to describe measurement uncertainties. The statistical definitions of a confidence interval requires that the errors come only from A distributions
- Therefore only the word interval (U) is used to define the region "about the measurement result that encompasses a large fraction $p$ of the probability distributions characterised by that result and its combined standard uncertainty and $p$ is the coverage probability or level of confidence of the interval"
# Comparing data - Standard normal distributions
- Enables data to be converted to a standard score or z-score $$P(x)=\frac{1}{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-0.5(\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma})^{2}}$$ $$P(z)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}e^{-0.5z^{2}}$$
- All other normal distributions are equivalent to this distribution when the independent variable is:
	- Shifted by the mean
	- Scaled by the standard deviation
	- And the probability density function is scaled by $\frac{1}{\sigma}$ $$z=\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}$$
# Student's -t
- A family of continuous probability distributions that arises when estimating the mean of a normally distributed population in situations where the sample size is small (inferential stats) and population standard deviation is unknown. It is an essential tool for estimating the level of confidence in the uncertainty
$$\pm t_{p}=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu}{\frac{s}{\sqrt{n}}}$$

## Interpreting Degrees of Freedom
- B DOF are difficult to estimate
- However, where the effective DOF > 10, the coverage factor is within ~6% (6% of k~2)
- 3 < 10 < 30 < 100
- Overestimating B by using worst case values is equivalent to having $v\to 1$. Using the resulting reducing factor will result in a large over-estimate of the standard uncertainty
# B Distributions
- Common error is to assume that because an instrument is used that produces reproducible results, it has no B error
- Examples: person doing experiment, instrument used, constant used, rounding used
- B come from an (often unseen) distribution - error sources about which very little is known. The difficulty is in choosing this distribution so that its effects can be appropriately handled
- B errors are independent of the number of samples taken (statistical averaging cannot reduce B errors)
- Manufacturers often specify parameters without a level of confidence, making it difficult to combine B uncertainties with A
# Assigning A or B Datasets
- If data is truly random (A):
	- It will change each time the measurement is repeated
	- Will spend equal amounts of the time on either side of the media
- For A: the data is randomised each time the measurement is repeated, given the standard deviation $s$ is determined, the uncertainty is given by $$ESDM=u(x) = \frac{s(x)}{\sqrt{n}}$$
- For B: the data is the same each time the measurement is repeated, uncertainty is given by $$u(x)=s(x)\neq ESDM$$
# Interpreting Manufacturers Data (B)
- Consider the parameters in a data sheet, it specifies parameters associated with the sensor, instrument or system
- Each parameter from the larger population is considered a random variable, but once the sensor, instrument or system is selected, there parameters are considered a fixed value $$MINMAX=4s(x), TYP=1.984s(x)$$
- If "excellent" then $DOF=v=100$

# The GUM Method
- 7 steps (principals)
1. Specify what is being measured. Defining input quantities $x_i$ and expressing measurand $Y$ as a model based on the inputs $$E[Y] = y=f(x_{1},x_{2},...,x_{N})$$ and define sensitivity coeffs $c_i$
2. Identify what causes the result to change. Create a list of all wanted and unwanted sources
3. Quantify the uncertainty components in terms of A and B. For each source of uncertainty we need 3 parameters: $U_{i}, k_{i}, v_{i}$ the distribution of each source may also need to be identified
4. Convert to standard uncertainties, $u(x_{i})=\frac{U_{i}}{k_{i}}$
5. Combining the uncertainties $u_{c}$, correlation between the various sources needs to be identified. The simplest cases are $\rho = (x_{i},x_{j})=-1, 0 or +1$
6. Create an expanded uncertainty, $U=ku_{c}(y)$ requiring calculation of $v_{eff}$
7. Clearly state the result, use $Y=\bar{y}\pm U$ a statement of units, coverage an dprobability (level of confidence)

# The Error List
- All sources of error that may affect the measurand should be listed in an uncertainty analysis table $(u(x_{i}), n, v)$, even if the effecs may be considered insignificant.
- 4 Components:
	- Calibration uncertainty - from manufacturing data, often $\pm (a+b\%)$ where $b$ is a variable or rang. If not stated, interpreted as expanded uncertainties at a 95% probability. If not provided, use judgement or attempt to find a reference and perform repeated measurements
	- Correction to uncertainty - ambient conditions of pressure, temp, humidity, drift since calibration (aging)
	- A Uncertainty of Mean- from $n$ readings, where $s$ is the standard deviation of individual readings
	- Resolution - instrument uncertainty of measurement, effectively a rounding error, typically included in the above, unless the number $n$ is small or no appreciable scatter in measured n-data points
		- Digital - rounding
		- Analogue - readabiloty
# Creating a New DOF after $u_c$
- What if all we have calculated is combined uncertainty:
$$u_{c}^{2}(y) = \Sigma^{N}_{i=1}u_{i}^{2}(y)=\Sigma^{N}_{i=1}(\frac{\delta f}{\delta x_{i}})^{2}u^{2}(x_{i}) \space | \space c_{i}=\frac{\delta f}{\delta x}|_{x=x_{i}}$$
- The effective DOF $v_{eff}$ is used to represent the eff-DOF of the uncertainty created from the model built from  a number of seperate input quantities ${x_{1},...x_{N}}$
- Estimated for uncorrelated using Welch Satterthwaite formula $$v_{eff}=\frac{u_{c}^{2}}{\frac{\Sigma^{N}_{i=1}|c_{i}u(x_{i})|^{4}}{v_{i}}}$$
- Due to 4th power, $v_{eff}$ will be dominated by sources with large $u(x_{i})$ especially with low DOF
- DOF $v_{eff}$ is required to determine the coverage $k$ using the students-t dist
- Rule of thumb for B coverage factors:
	- For rounding errors with rectangular dist set $U_{i}$ equal to half width of the distribution and $k=1.73$
	- In estimating DOF for B, ANMI suggests: rough=3, reasonable=10, good=30,excellent=100

# Determine Coverage Factors (A and B)
- A: with access to data, you can calc STDev and the ESDM, use ESDM as the standard uncertainty $u(x_{i})$
	- Calibraton uncertainties, where data is provided, use the stated expanded uncertainty and coverage factor. Where not stated, assume expanded uncertainty from the data with coverage $k=2$ (95% using Gaussian)
- B: use the Rules of thumb knowledge to estimate $v$ and use the students-t to find coverage $k$
# Confusion in Stating Uncertainty
- The GUM is based on standard $u(x_{i})$ combined $u_{c}$ and expanded $U$ uncertainties. These are positive quantities with NO SIGN
- In practice we expect uncertainties to be stated as a $\pm$ value
- To resolve confusion:
	- Give $Y=Y_{m}\pm U$ with units for $Y_{m}$ and $U$ stated
	- Both measurand $Y_m$ and expanded uncertainty $U$ should be rounded to same level
	- Level confidence stated
	- Coverage factor $k$ given
	- Avoid wors standard, combined or expanded when possible
