# Analog to Digital Converters (ADCs)
- Converts an input analog signal $x(t)$ into a discrete digital sequence $x[n]$
- Range of values for $x[n]$ (amplitude) is defined by number of sampling bits, N 
- $V_{ref}$ of ADC defines the Full Scale Range (FSR) which is digitised
- The maximum value is $x[n]_{max}=2^{N}$ which corresponds to the $FSR=V_{ref}$ 
- Input signal which is sampled is typically an analogue voltage
![[Pasted image 20261006131003.png]]
# Understanding ADCs
- Sampling Time = time required to sample and hold the signal $t_{s}$
- Number of Input Channels = most A/D converters have multiple input channels. The converter can usually be programmed to convert input voltages on all the channels, converting each separate voltage in rotation
- Resolution = the smallest change $\Delta V$ that can be accurately measured that will produce a measurable change in the output signal. In ADCs resolution is proportional to the number of bits of the analog to digital converter i.e. the more bits then the greater the resolution
$$\Delta V=\frac{V_{ref}}{(2^{N})-1}...N=8,V_{ref}=10V....\Delta V=39mV$$
- Signal conversion by an ADC introduces noise. Noise due to:
	- ADC resolution (amplitude noise)
	- Sampling period and conversion time noise (phase noise)

- Digital filters process signals in the time domain. Analog, CT signal first have to be sampled and digitised at discrete intervals using an analog-to-digital converter
- What would happen if an analog CT signal $x(t)$ at say 5kHz is sampled at 8kHz and then passed through a 5kHz low pass filter?$$x(t)=cos(\bar{\omega}_{M}t), \omega_{m} = 5kHz$$
	- Model sampling (fundamental):$$s(t)=cos(\bar{\omega_{S}t}), \omega_{s}=8kHz$$
- Mixing between the 5kHz signal and the 8kHz would cause signals to be generated at the sum and difference frequencies. Thus signals at 3kHz and 13kHz would be produced. When converting back to CT domain, the 13kHz signal would be outside the passband of the output filter, but the 3kHz signal would be inside the passband and thus appear at the output as an alias (noise)

# How do we Minimise/Quantify Aliasing
- Since aliasing involves a folding of energy or power from one part of the frequency domain to another, we need to develop an understanding of the tools to analyse the energy and power content in the frequency domain
	- We analyse these by looking at the signal in the frequency domain using Parseval's theorem which says the total power (or energy) is the same whether you analyse the system in the time domain or the frequency domain. Then we can apply this to understanding the controlling aliasing
- For periodic signals Parseval's Theorem allows us to calculate the power using the frequency domain using the relationship $$\bar{P}=\frac{1}{T}\int^{T}_{0}|x(t)|^{2}dt=\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}|a_{k}|^{2}$$
- The power in each harmonic is given $|a_{k}|^{2}$ (the amplitude). Fourier components are aperiodic

# Avoiding Aliasing/Nyquist Rate
- Consider the sampled signal $x_{s}(t)$ for which
	- The original signal $x(t)$ is band-limited (the maximum frequency is limited)
	- The signal bandwidth is smaller than the sampling frequency $\omega_{s}$ 
- Note, the bandwidth is the total width (from most negative frequency to maximum positive frequency) of the signal
- A signal $x(t)$ is band-limited if: $$|X(j\bar{\omega})|=0 \space for \space |\bar{\omega}|>\bar{\omega}_{M}$$
![[Pasted image 20261006141001.png]]
- Signal bandwidth for a base-band signal (one centered around DC) is twice the maximum frequency of the signal or $B_{\omega }=2\omega_{M}$
- The Nyquist rate is the sampling frequency that must be exceed to ensure an alias-free spectrum, and is given by:$$\bar{\omega}_{Nyq}=2\bar{\omega}_{max}=4\pi f_{max}$$ or $$f_{Nyq}=2f_{max}$$
- Note that sampling at the Nyquist rate is not sufficient to ensure there is no aliasing. We must sample at greater than then Nyquist rate to avoid aliasing, the sampling theorem requires that $$\bar{\omega}_{sampling}>\bar{\omega}_{Nyq}=2\bar{\omega}_max$$
![[Pasted image 20261006141525.png]]
# Anti-Aliasing Filter
- A signal $x(t)$ needs to be band-limited before it is sampled to avoid aliasing. A base-band signal (centered around DC or zero frequency) which is not band-limited needs to be low-pass filtered first in order to restrict the signal bandwidth. The filter which performs this task is known as the anti-aliasing filter
![[Pasted image 20261006141738.png]]
- In practice, filters with step-like responses (ideal) cannot be realised. Further, filters with very sharp edges must be avoided as they cause ringing in the time domain. Practical filters attempt to make the signal components above $\omega_{M}$ roll off in a smooth fashion
![[Pasted image 20261006141915.png]]
### Parseval's Theorem for Energy Signals
- For energy signals (aperiodic and therefore handled with a Fourier transform) the total energy in the time domain signal $x(t)$ is equal to the integral of the energy in the (continuous) Fourier spectrum. The total energy in the signal is defined as $$E=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}|x(t)|^{2}dt=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}|X(f)|^{2}dt$$
- We can treat $|x(t)|^{2}$ as the instantaneous power of the signal $x(t)$
- Parseval's Theorem allows us to calculate this energy using the frequency domain information using the relationship 
- Where the term $|X(f)|^{2}$ is called the energy spectra density (in J/Hz)
- A signal which has a finite energy E has an average power $f(t)$ of zero. Such a signal is called an energy signal. 
![[Pasted image 20261006142439.png]]
### Parseval's Theorem for Periodic Signals
- For periodic signals, we cannot define the total energy of the signal, but we can define the average power over one period as $$\bar{P}=\frac{1}{T}\int^\frac{T}{2}_{-T/2}|x_{i}(t)|^{2}dt$$
- We can treat $|x(t)|^{2}$ as the instantaneous power of the signal $x(t)$
- The Fourier transform is not defined for periodic signals. For this we use the Fourier series which define the coefficients $$a_{k}=\frac{1}{T}\int^\frac{T_{x}}{2}_{\frac{T_{x}}{2}}x(t)e^{-ej\omega t}dt$$
- Therefore, Parseval's Theorem allows us to calculate the power using the frequency domain information using the relationship $$\bar{P}=\frac{1}{T}\int^{T}_{0}|x(t)|^{2}dt=\sum^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}|a_{k}|^{2}$$
- The power in each harmonic is given by $|a_{k}|^{2}$
