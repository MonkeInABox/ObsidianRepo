# Random Signals
- 4 types of statistical functions used to describe:
1. Mean squared-values $\bar{x^{2}}$ and the variance $\sigma^{2}$ - they provide information about the amplitude of the signal (summary statistics)
2. Probability distributions - they provide information about the statistical properties of the signal in the amplitude domain
3. Correlation functions - they provide information about the statistical properties of the signal in the time domain
4. Spectral density functions - they provide information about the statistical properties of the signal in the frequency domain

# Examples of Dynamic Signals
- Static signals do not change with time (or change so slowly they are considered constant). They are sometimes referred to as DC signals
- Dynamic signals are time dependent. Deterministic signals change in a predictable manner. Periodic signals (such as a sine wave) have a magnitude that repeats at regular intervals over time. Non-deterministic signals (random or noisy) have no discernible pattern or repetition and cannot be described using any function before it occurs (not predictable). However, certain characteristics of these signals may be known in advance using a statistical description
![[Pasted image 20260922122143.png]]
# Define Time Series
- A time series is a set of statistics, usually collected at regular intervals $$X_{1},X_{2},...X_{N}$$
- Time series data have a temporal ordering (compared to random order or spatial order)
- Time series analysis refers to problems in which observations are collected at regular time intervals and there are correlations among successive observations
	- Usually the intent is to discern whether there is some pattern in the values collected to date, with the intention of short term forecasting
- Standard analysis involve:
	- Smoothing/trend assessment
	- Assessment of correlation
	- Spectral analysis (Fourier)
# Classes of Deterministic Signals
## Energy Signals
- Subclass: Aperiodic signals
- Well behaved if amplitude $\to 0$ as $t \to \infty$  
- Definition: $$Energy=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}|x(t)|^{2}dt$$ for all time
- Energy is finite (a constant), power is 0
- Energy signals $x(t)$ are of limited (finite) time duration
- E.g. non-periodic signals (pulses) are energy signals
## Power Signals
- Subclass: Periodic signals (if finite energy over period $T$) $sin[3\pi \frac{t}{4}]$
- Definition:$$Average Power=\bar{P}=lim_{T\to\infty}\frac{1}{T}\int^{\frac{T}{2}}_\frac{-T}{2}|x_{i}(t)|^{2}dt$$ for all time
- Power is finite, energy is undefined ($\infty$)
- Take limits at $T\to \infty$ large where $T>T_0$ where $T_{0}$ is the longest repeating period of the signal of interest or $T_{0}=\frac{1}{f_{0}}$ where $f_{0}$ is the lowest frequency component
- E.g. periodic signals are power signals

## Fourier Series
- Any periodic signal $x(t)$ with period $T$ can be presented by a Fourier series as $$x(t)=A_{0}+\Sigma^{\infty}_{n=1}A_{k}cos(k\omega_{0}t)+B_{k}sin(k\omega_{0}t)$$
- Where $T = \frac{2\pi}{\omega_{0}}$, this form uses a series of sines and cosines where each sine or cosine is weighted by factor $A_{k}$ or $B_{k}$, the Fourier series coefficients at each harmonic frequency $(k\omega_{0})$0 given by:
![[Pasted image 20260922125217.png|209]]
- We can also write this in terms of phase as: $$x(t)=A_{0}+\Sigma^{\infty}_{k=1}C_{k}cos(k\omega_{0}t-\psi_{n})$$
- Since sine and cosine can be expressed in terms of complex exponentials, we can write the FS in complex form as $$x(t)=\Sigma^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}a_{k}e^{jk\omega_{0}t}$$
- Complex coefficients given as $a_{k}=\frac{1}{T}\int^{\frac{T}{2}}_\frac{-T}{2} x(t)e^{jk\omega_{0}t}dt$ 
- The continuous time FS is given for a periodic signal in time $x(t)$ as:$$x(t)=\Sigma^{\infty}_{k=-\infty}a_{k}e^{jk\omega_{0}t}$$ with FS components given by:$$a_{k}=\frac{1}{T}\int^{\frac{T}{2}}_\frac{-T}{2} x(t)e^{jk\omega_{0}t}dt$$ where $T=\frac{2\pi}{\omega_{0}}|\omega_{0}=2\pi f_{0}$ 
- Since we need period $T$, FS are applied to periodic signals
- Fourier components are aperiodic (non repeating) and discrete
# Comparing the Fourier Series and Fourier Transform
- For a periodic signal $x(t)$ of period $T_{0}$ spectral analysis uses the FS
![[Pasted image 20260922130234.png]]
- For an aperiodic signal $x(t)$ spectral analysis uses the FT as $$x(t)=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}X(f)e^{j2\pi ft}df \space | \space X(f)=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}x(t)e^{-j2\pi ft}dt$$![[Pasted image 20260922130223.png]]
- Difference to FS: integration limits, spectral components are continuous $a_{k}\to X(f)$

# Transfer Function and Bandwidth
- When dealing with signals and data, in the frequency domain, we are typically interested in the bandwidth of signals. Bandwidth is measured using signal power, hence we deal with $|X(f)|^{2}$ where $bandwidth=f_{3dBMAX}-f_{3dBMIN}$
- Signals and data often have different magnitudes, for example the FT of an exponential s a Lorentzian![[Pasted image 20260922131649.png]]
- Since the magnitude varies with the constant $\alpha$ (decay rate) we use the transfer function to normalise the response as $T(f)=\frac{X(f)}{X(f_{0})}$
- Where $f_{0}$ is the centre frequency. For signals centred around $f_{0}=0$ (DC) we have no oscillatory component in the time domain, we normalise to $T(f)=\frac{X(f)}{X(0)}$. To find bandwidth use $|T(f)|^{2}$ ![[Pasted image 20260922131920.png]]

# Parseval's Theorem for Energy Signals
- The total energy in the time domain signal $x(t)$ is equal to the integral to the integral of the energy in the continuous Fourier spectrum. The total energy in the signal is defined as $$E=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}|x(t)|^{2}dt$$
- We can treat $|x(t)|^{2}$ as the instantaneous power of the signal $x(t)$
- P's theorem allows us to calculate the energy using the frequency domain information using$$E=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}|x(t)|^{2}dt=\int^{\infty}_{-\infty}|X(f)|^{2}df$$
- Area gives energy![[Pasted image 20260922140235.png]]
