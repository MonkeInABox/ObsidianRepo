# Virtue Ethics 
- Resurrected in 1970s as a reaction against a legalistic approach to ethics
- A virtue-based moral theory, in addition to or instead of the deontological and utilitarian approaches
- Making good or sound decisions involves the exercise of judgement 
- Those who emphasise virtue, argue that moral rules do not solve real moral problems because they must always be applied, and any application requires judgement, which cannot be automatic, and which requires a virtuous person to make the judgement correctly
- Consistent with Aristotelian approach that guiding the purpose of life should be the quest for goodness and virtue
- Two basic kinds:
	- Intellectual virtues: the highest of which is wisdom, result from the proper functioning of the higher or intellectual aspects of human beings
	- Moral virtues: derive from the proper control by reason of the bodily appetites and inclinations. We are not virtuous by practice
- Man is the measure of things - Anthropocentric view
- Whereas consequentialism and deontology determine what makes a right act right; virtue ethics takes a normative stance on how to go about achieving something of moral worth
- What is right is that that would be carried out by virtuous person
- Ethics can be taught, but ethical conduct cannot be practiced
- All the theoretical knowledge in the world about ethics will not make you a virtuous person/manager/marketer unless you practice
- Concentrates on the person who performs the act
- Particularly, focuses on the character traits of the person or the character development of the person 
- The possession and development of a good character of the moral virtues will lead a person to do the right thing
# Egoism (Max Stirner)
- Individualistic, self-centred
- Strong sense of self to exclusion of others
- Strong notion of independence
- All activities directed toward self-satisfaction, not God or anyone else
- Environment - the earth belongs to those who know how to use it
- Others exist for common use of the other
- Often referred to as individualistic anarchism
# Humanism
- Three principal theses:
	- Recognition of the equal dignity of everyone
	- Altruism (elevation of the particular human being other than me)
	- Preference for freedom of action
- Humanism created based on the interaction of these three
	- My freedom cannot be enjoyed at expense of your freedom or dignity
	- My autonomy is limited by equally in the community in which I belong
- Humanism provides for the promotion of humanity
# Relativism (Moral)
- Moral values are relative to a particular environment, they are not universal and are not absolute
- Builds upon notion, When in Rome do as the Romans do
- A view about the domain over which any moral position ranges
- Relativism stands in opposition to absolutism
- Also doesn't negate 'moral pluralism' - multiple views

# How (Ethical Theories Test) EXAM
- Develop a list of questions that reflect various ethical theories that can aid managers in determining whether a particular contemplated action is ethical or not
- Recognise that there are sometimes conflicts among various ethical principles that imply different and sometimes competing positions. Number of conflicts increases as number of stakeholders increases. 
1. Does action violate the law? (Legal)
2. Is action contrary to widely accept moral obligations? (Duties)
3. Is it likely that any major damages to people or organisations will result from the contemplated action? (Consequences)
4. Is there a satisfactory alternative action that produces equal or greater benefits to the parties affected than the proposed action? (Utilitarian)
5. Does action violate any other special duties? (Special obligations)
6. Is the intent of the contemplated action harmful? (Motives)
7. Does contemplated action infringe on the rights of consumer or stakeholders in any way? (Rights)
8. Does the contemplated action leave another person less well-off? (Justice)
9. Can the contemplated action be considered sustainable in that it does not negatively impact upon future generation? (Sustainable)

# Tutorial
**Context**: You discover your company's resume-screening AI systematically downranks candidates from certain universities and neighborhoods, which correlates with race and class. Fixing it will delay the product launch and cost you a promotion.
**Virtue Ethics**: Demands courage to speak up and justice to correct the bias.
**Moral Relativism**: Might note that if unconscious bias is pervasive in hiring anyway, the AI just reflects existing practices. Or that different cultures have different views
**Humanism**: Strongly opposes this as it denies equal opportunity and dignity to certain groups.
**Egoism**: Complex calculation  speaking up risks promotion, but if the bias becomes public, association with it could damage career.

**Context**: You're a software engineer at an automotive company. Management asks you to write code that makes vehicles perform better on emissions tests than in real-world driving
**Virtue Ethics**: A virtuous engineer would refuse because deception corrupts character, regardless of consequences. 
**Moral Relativism**: Considers the cultural/corporate context.
**Humanism**: Would reject this because it harms public health and violates trust, treating people as means rather than ends.
**Egoism**: Evaluates based on self-interest. Might initially seem appealing (keep job, get bonus), but rational egoism would recognize long-term risks: legal liability, career damage, guilt.

## Case study 1
Engineers are always constrained by budget. What responsibility does an engineer have regarding the design of safe structures in a developing country, where funds are usually not available to engineer structures to standards common in more developed nations? How does an engineer ethically develop a design when the budget is so severely constrained?
- That country's standards at least
- Education for maintenance
- Design suited for the local context
- Transparent with what it costs and what is needed - what can and can't you do
- Cost effective materials - local
- Prioritise safety - delivering on core objective and not compromising on any safety considerations
- Scope change
- Utilitarian - minimum product that still delivers the objective - biggest win - greatest number of people
