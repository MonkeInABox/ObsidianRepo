# What is Reliability
- When designing/developing an asset:
	- What function does it need to provide
	- What is the required reliability
	- Who benefits
	- Who may be involved in its life
	- What risks are involved
	- What is the cycle cost
	- Who pays for it
> [!NOTE] Reliability Definitions
> The probability that an item can perform its intended function for a specified interval under stated conditions; ability of an item to perform a required function under given conditions for a given time interval
- The importance of defining function:
	- All items are designed to perform one or more required functions
	- Some are active, some are passive
- Quality vs. Reliability
	- To be reliable: must do more than meet an initial factory or quality specification, operate satisfactorily for a specified period of time in the actual application for which it was intended, conformity to its specification
	- Reliability is the ability to continue to comply with its specification over its useful life
- Reliability engineering (order of priority)
1. Apply engineering knowledge and specialist techniques to prevent or to reduce the likelihood or frequency of failures
2. To identify and correct the causes of failures that do occur, despite the efforts to prevent them
3. To determine ways of coping with failures that do occur, if their causes have not been corrected
4. To apply methods for estimating the likely reliability of new designs, and for analysing reliability data
- Why do engineering items fail
	- The design might be inherently incapable
	- The item might be overstressed
	- Failures might be caused by:
		- Variation
		- Wear out
		- Time-dependent mechanisms
		- Sneaks
		- Errors

# Systems and Functions
- Structural: the physical structure of the subsystems and components of a system
- Functional: various functions of the system and how these functions are fulfilled
- When considering operation and reliability, we tend to have more focus on functional considerations
- When considering manufacturing and maintenance, we tend to have more focus on structural considerations

> [!NOTE] Function
> What the owner or user of a physical asset or system wants it to do

- A failure is the loss of ability to perform as required

# Failures and Failures Modes

> [!NOTE] Failure
>Loss of ability to perform as required


> [!NOTE] Fault
> Inability to perform as required, due to an internal state

- When defining a failure:
	- The failure criteria should be unambiguous
	- Should be related to a measurable parameter or clear indication
	- Recognise there can be subjective variations in assessing failures but we should try and minimise this
> Failure Mode: Manner in which a failure occurs
- Can be categorised as: 
	- Desired function is not obtained
	- Specified function lost or outside of operational limits
	- Failure indication is observed but there is not immediate or critical impact on the equipment-unit function

> Failure Cause: Set of circumstances that leads to failure

> Failure Effect: Consequence of failure mode in terms of the operation, functions or status of the item

> Failure Mechanism: Process that leads to failure

> Common Mode Failures: Failures of different items characterised by the same failure mode


> [!NOTE] Errors
> Discrepancy between a computed, observed or measured value or condition and the true, specified or theoretically correct value or condition


# Failure Mode and Mechanisms
- Mechanisms are physical processes whose occurrence either lead to or are caused by stress agents (challenges) and may deteriorate the capacity of an item
![[Pasted image 20260318094132.png]]
- Mechanical failure mechanisms
	- Stress induced: cause or are the result of localised stress (elastic deformation, brittle fracture)
	- Strength-reduced: lead to a reduction of an item's strength or endurance to withstand stress or damage (radiation embrittlement, corrosion)
	- Stress-increasing: results in an increase in the applied stress (fatigue, thermal shock)
- Electrical failure mechanisms
	- Electrical stress: when subjected to voltage/current/electrostatic discharge levels higher than design
	- Intrinsic: related to the basic electrical activity of the device
	- Extrinsic: stem from issues with packaging and interconnections

# Reliability Basics
- Focus areas for reliability engineering
![[Pasted image 20260318094631.png]]
- A system is a collection of components that provides a function
- The line between component and system is arbitrary and depends on objectives, scope, state and conventions
- Hardware domain
	- Component reliability
		- Failure modes
		- Failure mechanisms
		- Failure probability model
	- System reliability
		- Functional model
		- Failure/success logic model
		- Dependencies between components
		- Failure mechanisms
		- Probability models
- Software domain
	- Failures due to variability can not occur
	- Does not degrade
	- Function of machine environment often
- Human factors domain
	- Skill based: inattention and over attention
	- Rule based: misapplication 
	- Knowledge based
- Conceptual failure models
	- Failures are due to a complex set of interactions between
		- The stresses that act on and within the system
		- The materials/elements of the system
	- 4 conceptual stress models
		- Stress-Strength: item fails `iff` the stress exceeds the strength
		- Damage-endurance: stress causes damage that accumulates irreversibly. Item fails when damage exceeds endurance
		- Challenged-response: element is bad but only when the element is challenged does it reveal itself to be bad
		- Tolerance-equipment: performance is satisfactory if its tolerance remains within requirement
- Fusion models: combining physics of failure and data driven models

# Reliability Block Models
- Series system
![[Pasted image 20260318095412.png]]
$$P(Unit1 \cap Unit2\space survive) = P(Unit1Survives) \times P(Unit2Survives)=R_{1}\times R_{2}$$
$$R_{system}= \Pi^{n}_{i=1}R_{i}$$
	- For systems comprised of two or more independent components in series, you can use the exponential to calculate reliability
$$R_{sys} = e^{-\lambda_{1}t}\times e^{-\lambda_{2}t} = e^{-(\lambda_{1}+\lambda_{2})t}$$

# Introduction to Time to Failure
- Reliability of non-repairable items
	- We only study the item to its first failure occurs
	- Assume that the item is either discarded or if not, we are not interested in its subsequent
- Time to failure is a function of $T$
	- Non-negative
	- Continuously distributed random variable with PDF $f(t)$
- Life distributions
	- Exponential, Weibull, Normal, Log Normal, Gamma
![[Pasted image 20260318100414.png]]
$$R(t)=P(T > t)=\int^{\infty}_{t}f(x)dx$$

# Hazard Rate/Failure Rate Function
- $r(t)$ or $h(t)$
- Conditional probability that an item fails during interval $\delta t$, given it has survived time $t$
$$r(t)=\frac{f(t)}{1-F(t)}=\frac{f(t)}{R(t)}$$

# Exponential Distribution and MTTF
- What is a constant failure rate model
	- Many items/components exhibit constant hazard rates for a significant proportion of their life
$$\lambda = FailureRate = \frac{1}{MTTF}$$
$$\mu = MTTF = \int^{\infty}_{0}R(t)dt = \int^{\infty}_{0}e^{-\lambda t}dt=\frac{1}{\lambda}$$
$$Failure = F(t) = 1- R(t) = 1-e^{-\lambda t} = 1- e^{\frac{-t}{MTTF}}$$
![[Pasted image 20260318101214.png]]
![[Pasted image 20260318101239.png]]

- Inverse relationship between MTTF and $\lambda$ is only valid for exponential distribution
- Memoryless Properties:
	- Plot of hazard rate for an exponential distribution is a horizontal line
	- Probability of failing in next instant given it has survived thus far is the same as previously
	- Chance of survival is independent of age
